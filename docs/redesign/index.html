Three ways out of the wireframe.

The shipped frontend is not badly designed so much as under-designed: a sound system, described in docs/DESIGN.md, that the screens only half implement. What follows is the diagnosis from the deployed pages, then three complete directions, each of which commits to something the others do not. They are proposals to choose between, not three shades of the same idea.

What is actually wrong

Measured against the deployed build at 1440 px and at 853 px, which is a 1280 px projector at the 150% zoom the demo runs at. Every number below is from those pages, not an impression.

  1. No composition anywhere. Every page is one centred sheet holding a top-to-bottom stack of heading, paragraph, list. There is no second column, no full-bleed, no asymmetry, and no page whose shape differs from any other page's.
  2. The document metaphor is invisible. The design system says Cekgu is a review document on a desk. #fbfaf7 on #f1efea is a two percent luminance step, so the sheet never reads as an object and the desk never reads as a surface.
  3. The hero's right half is empty. Roughly 45% of the hero panel is blank, and the marketing site shows no picture of the product at any point. A tool whose pitch is "two readers disagreed with your key, here is the receipt" proves none of it on screen.
  4. The record workspace repeats itself. A five-option disposition form and a Record Decision button render on all twelve items, open, always — including the nine that need no decision. The sample record is 5 076 px tall and most of that is identical chrome.
  5. A Clear item and a Key Error item look the same. Same type size, same row height, same controls. The one distinction is a small chip at the row's right edge, which is the last place the eye lands.
  6. Every button has the same weight. A 36 px bordered rectangle at 4 px radius, up to fourteen of them on one screen, so nothing on the page is more important than anything else.
  7. Sign-in is an unstyled form. A 575 px form floating at the top left of a 1130 px panel, with roughly 400 px of empty ground beneath it. It is the second screen in the demo.
  8. No motion, no depth, no image, no texture. Nothing on any screen moves, nothing casts a shadow that means anything, and the only artwork in the product is the mascot.

One thing worth defending

The system underneath is genuinely good and it is not generated-looking. Paper for what a person wrote, grotesque for what Cekgu says, mono for what a machine issued; five verdict hues chosen for what each asks of the reader; round for bubbles and square for paper. All three directions keep that. The problem is that the screens spend none of it.

And one risk in the current palette

Warm off-white ground plus a serif body sits one step away from the most saturated look in AI-generated design right now — the warm cream editorial page. Cekgu escapes it today on two technicalities: the serif is body copy rather than a display face, and the accent is a crimson pen rather than terracotta. That is a narrow margin, and each direction below widens it differently: A darkens the ground away from cream, B leaves the light palette to the marketing site, C answers with saturated colour.

The three directions

Each is a full landing page, sign-in screen and record workspace, built with the product's real copy and the real sample paper. Open them at a wide window; they are also correct at 853 px.

Instrument

Direction C

Two Readers

Cekgu owns a shape nobody else in the category owns, and used it at 24 px in the corner of the navigation. Here the answer bubble is the entire visual language, and the two cats stop being one stage on one screen and become the product's second visual system.

Commits to
One atom, and colour. The five verdicts become a palette rather than five chips.
Signature
Two rings on one bubble: inner is reader A, outer is reader B, agreement at a glance.
Since chosen
Landing rebuilt on SolarSim's structure with a generated hero video; four routes folded into one page; topbar and drawer on the app.
Risk
The boldest, and the one that most changes what Cekgu looks like from across a room.

Choosing between them

  A · Marked Paper B · Instrument C · Two Readers
What a judge sees first A marked exam paper. The product, immediately. A working instrument with live readings and receipts. A confident brand shape and five colours with names.
Serves the track brief Request ids on the paper's margin, in the hero. Strongest. Ids are a standing fixture, and requested-versus-served is printed. Ids in a receipt card, one step back from the hero.
Distance from the current build Smallest. Same tokens, deepened; mostly layout and one new component. Largest. A dark theme the app does not currently default to, and a new table. Middle. Same light ground, new component language throughout.
Cost to implement before 5 Sept Lowest. The pen ring is one SVG; the rest is CSS the system already implies. Highest. Every screen is re-laid out, and dark contrast needs re-checking. Medium. The atom is one component reused at three scales.
Where it is weakest Least surprising. It improves the existing idea rather than proposing a new one. A teacher is not an operator; density can read as intimidating to the actual user. Colour fields are the furthest from "quiet tool", and the most fashion-dependent.

If you want a recommendation

A for the product, B's record workspace inside it. A wins the first five seconds with the audience that matters, a judge who has never seen the tool, because it shows a marked exam paper rather than an interface. But B's reading matrix is the better answer to the one screen the demo turns on: twelve questions, two readers and the key on screen at once beats scrolling, and it is the clearest picture of the multi-model consensus the track is judging. They compose — A's desk and margin will hold B's matrix without argument. C is the strongest brand and the weakest fit for someone reviewing a paper at 11 pm; its bubble atom is worth stealing for the summary row whichever direction wins.
